# A common-factor restriction across three Pascal rows

Let $a\ge10^{128}$ occur in proper columns $(3,5,7)$ or $(5,6,7)$. In **each** of those three rows choose a nonempty proper subset of its numerator terms, and form the three products. **At least one prime greater than seven occurs with different exponents in these products.** Thus, after deleting all factors of 2, 3, 5 and 7, the three products cannot all be equal. This statement uses just the three indicated representations.

For the four-column patterns $(3,5,7,8)$ and $(5,6,7,8)$ the result holds **at every height**, since the earlier finite exclusion forces a common value above $10^{128}$. It gives a description of how the primes in a hypothetical example must be distributed. Make one vertex for each numerator term in the four rows. For each prime $p>7$ dividing $a$, join the four terms it divides, one in each row. **The resulting graph is connected.** [Finite height exclusion](Erdos849PatternHeights.md) · [Every numerator term meets a prime above seven](Erdos849LargePrimeIsolates.md)

Indeed if the graph on the first three rows had two components each containing a prime, take the positions of one component in each row. These are nonempty proper subsets. Every prime above seven would occur either in all three products with exponent $v_p(a)$, or in none, contradicting the theorem. The independent exclusion of terms supported on primes through seven ensures that every vertex meets a prime. Each vertex in the eighth column therefore joins the connected graph formed by the first three columns.

In the multiplicative group of positive rationals modulo the subgroup generated by $2,3,5,7$, the intersection of the four subgroups generated by each row's numerator terms is exactly the infinite cyclic subgroup generated by $[a]$. To see this, express any common element separately in each row, assigning integer exponents to its terms. A prime $p>7$ occurs at its four positions with the same positive valuation $v_p(a)$. The four assigned exponents must therefore agree. Connectedness forces every position to have the same exponent. Conversely the product of one whole row represents $[a]$, since the omitted factorial has only small prime factors. The presence of a prime above seven gives $[a]$ infinite order.

## An almost equal pair of rational numbers

Write $K$ for one of the triples, $n_k$ for the row in column $k$, $S_k$ for the selected offsets, $s_k=|S_k|$, and $X_k=\prod_{i\in S_k}(n_k-i)$. Put $\alpha_k=s_k/k$. The three column indices are pairwise coprime. The primitive integer coefficients $c_k$ satisfying $\sum c_k=\sum c_k\alpha_k=0$ are unique up to sign. None vanishes, and each $c_k\alpha_k$ is an integer. Define

$$W=\prod_{k\in K} X_k^{c_k}(k!)^{-c_k\alpha_k}.$$

The moment calculation proves $W\ne1$ for every choice of subsets when $a\ge10^{128}$. Its reason is that the first nonzero term in each of three Taylor expansions decays at a distinct rate. The slowest one dominates the sum of the other two throughout this range. [Proof and exact dominance tests](Erdos849ComponentMoments.md)

If the three products have the same exponents outside $P=\{2,3,5,7\}$, write $W=\prod_{p\in P}p^{b_p}$. This follows from $\sum c_k=0$ and the small prime factors of $k!$. Let $t=\log_{10}a$ and put $d_3=1,d_5=d_6=2,d_7=3$. Since $(k!a)^{1/k}$ is the geometric mean of the $k$ terms, $n_k-k+1>d_k a^{1/k}$ for this range. Moreover

$$\left|\log X_k-\alpha_k\log(k!a)\right|\le\frac{s_k(k-s_k)}{n_k-k+1}.$$

Indeed subtracting the logarithms of selected and complementary terms pairwise and summing, with weight $1/k$, gives the expression on the left before absolute values. In particular $|\log W|<4000a^{-1/7}$ by the finite coefficient check, hence $|W-1|<10^5a^{-1/7}$.

For each prime $p$, its full power $p^e\Vert a$ divides at least one term in the seventh-column numerator. Removing a term of maximum valuation leaves at most $\lfloor 7/p^j\rfloor$ multiples of $p^j$ for every $j$. Thus the total valuation left is at most $v_p(7!)$. In particular $p^e\le n_7<5a^{1/7}$ and $e\le t$. We have $0\le v_p(X_k)\le e+v_p(k!)$. The verifier checks $\sum |c_k|\le300$ and $v_p(k!)\le4$ here, so $|b_p|<1000t$. These estimates require no prime-power interpolation for the four-column patterns.

## Excluding a product supported on the four small primes

Apply the real-field form of Matveev's theorem to $W-1$. At most four positive rational primes occur, each with modified height less than 2. The theorem gives $\log|W-1|>-10^{15}(1+\log(1000t))$. The bound from the previous section implies $\log|W-1|<-t/5$ for $t\ge10^{22}$. At $10^{22}$ the lower estimate is already incompatible with this, and $t/(1+\log(1000t))$ is increasing. Therefore $t<10^{22}$. [Matveev's bound in Bugeaud, Mignotte and Siksek, Theorem 9.4](https://samirsiksek.github.io/siksek.github.io/papers/fibannalsfinal.pdf)

The remaining ranges are checked with exact four-dimensional lattices. On $L\le t\le U$ take the largest $e$ with $p^{7e}\le5^7 10^U$, or the coarser bound $e\le U$ in the first large range. For each subset-size triple this bounds $b_p=\sum c_k v_p(X_k)-\sum c_k\alpha_k v_p(k!)$ between explicit endpoints. Write $B_p$ for the maximum absolute endpoint.

Let $C$ be the scale in the table, $w=(1,2,3,3)$, and $h_p$ the nearest integer to $C\log p$. A putative nonzero vector $((w_p b_p)_{p\in P},\sum b_ph_p)$ has squared length at most

$$\sum_p (w_p B_p)^2+\left(\tfrac12\sum_p B_p+\sum_{k\in K}\frac{C|c_k|s_k(k-s_k)}{d_k\,10^{\lfloor L/k\rfloor}}\right)^2.$$

The supplied integer basis changes are checked to be unimodular. The minimum squared Gram–Schmidt length gives a lower bound on the squared length of *every* nonzero vector. Exact rational arithmetic shows the upper bound is smaller for every one of the 48 and 120 subset-size triples respectively. The ratios below are rounded down.
