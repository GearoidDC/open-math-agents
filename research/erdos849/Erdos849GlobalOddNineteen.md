# A squarefree-part bound for every five-row entry

Let $a$ occur in at least five distinct admissible rows of Pascal's triangle, and let $d$ be its largest proper column. Then **at least seven distinct primes greater than $\max(19,d)$ occur to odd exponents in $a$**. In particular the squarefree part of $a$ is at least

$$23\cdot29\cdot31\cdot37\cdot41\cdot43\cdot47=63{,}392{,}725{,}189.$$

Therefore every positive integer $b s^2$ with $b<63{,}392{,}725{,}189$ is excluded from five rows, regardless of the size of $s$. The existence of a five-row entry remains open.

## Which columns need to be checked

The earlier five-row search gives $a>10^{128}$. There are at least four distinct proper columns, and at most one belongs to $\{2,3,4,6\}$. The fixed-pair classifications force $d\ge8$ and give columns 5, 7 and 8 if $d=8$. If $9\le d\le16$, at least one earlier column $k$ satisfies $7\le k<d$. When $d=9$ and column 8 is absent, both 5 and 7 must be present. These facts follow by counting the remaining possible columns in $\{5,7,\ldots,d-1\}$. [Finite search and fixed-pair inputs](Erdos849Focused128.md)

## Finite part for columns 8 through 16

Assume that at most six primes above 19 occur to odd exponents. Write $a=\binom nd$. Each prime above 19 can divide at most one member of $n,n-1,\ldots,n-d+1$ and survives division by $d!$. At least $d-6$ of those integers consequently have squarefree part composed solely of primes through 19. When their offsets are arranged in increasing order, two of them are separated by at most $\lfloor(d-1)/(d-7)\rfloor$. The squarefree part of their product is a divisor of $9{,}699{,}690$, the product of the primes through 19.

For each eligible offset pair $i<j$ the verifier uses the necessary equation

$$(2n-i-j)^2-D y^2=(j-i)^2,$$

where $D$ is that squarefree part. It lists all starting solutions and follows the Pell recurrences modulo primes. Row residues survive only if their binomial coefficient also occurs in column $k$ modulo the test prime. Each surviving row is tested by exact integer binomial membership. This method includes $D=1$, for which the equation factors over the integers. Independent small-row controls recover the complete list of relevant pairs by direct factoring. [Pell construction and controls](Erdos849WiderOdd.md)

For $d=8$ we used column 7 and tested through $10^{350000}$. For every $9\le d\le16$ we tested through $10^{1000000}$, using every $k$ from 7 to $d-1$. The final exact tests excluded all five-row candidates in these ranges.

## Large values in the same columns

For an ordered set $K$ of represented columns, consider products obtained by choosing an even number of numerator terms in each column. These choices form a vector space over $\mathbb F_2$ of dimension $\sum_{k\in K}(k-1)$. The explicit height calculation proves that above the indicated cutoff no nonempty selection has square product. For an even/even pair the product of both full rows is a fixed nonsquare times $a^2$; the verifier checks the nonsquare constant in each applicable case. The resulting map into rational square classes is injective. For $d\le16$ its image lies in the space generated by the eight primes through 19 and those primes above 19 having odd exponent in $a$. Thus dimension at least 15 forces at least seven such primes. [Height proof and coefficient bounds](Erdos849HigherSquare.txt)

## Columns 17 and above

Put $m=n-d+1>d$. If $m>d^2$, Corollary 2 of Mukhopadhyay and Shorey gives eight distinct primes greater than $d$ occurring to odd exponents in the product of these $d$ consecutive terms, since $d\ge17$. They survive division by $d!$. If $m\le d^2$, every prime greater than $d$ dividing that product occurs just once, as its largest term is below $(d+1)^2$. For $d\le36$, this row size would give $a\le\binom{1331}{36}<10^{128}$. For $37\le d\le293$, formula (5) of the same paper gives eight terms containing a prime above $d$, using the exact prime counts in the verifier. For larger $d$, formula (3) applies without exceptions. The primes are distinct, since the terms form a block of length $d$. For $d=17,18$ only the prime 19 can be lost upon requiring a prime above 19. For $d\ge19$ all eight already exceed 19. [Mukhopadhyay and Shorey, formulas (3), (5), and Corollary 2](https://publi.math.unideb.hu/paper/924/download/10_5486_PMD_2004_2881.pdf)
