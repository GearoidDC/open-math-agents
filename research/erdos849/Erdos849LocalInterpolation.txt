Full prime powers in a common value of columns 7 and 8

Suppose a=C(n,8)=C(m,7)>=X=10^128, with n>=16 and m>=14.
For every full prime power p^e||a,

  p^e < p^v_p(420) * 46862860 * (a/X)^(1/16).

In particular a has at least 17 distinct prime divisors. The assertion
applies to any such two rows, irrespective of the other representations.

1. Local branches

For each row N, column k, and P=p^e||a there is an offset s in [0,k-1]
with P dividing N-s. In the factorial floor formula for v_p(C(N,k)),
exactly e levels contribute 1. At some level j>=e this gives
N mod p^j < k mod p^j <=k, hence N mod P < k. We use these offsets s,r
for columns 8,7 respectively. If e<=max(v_p(v):1<=v<=7), then P<=7
and the displayed inequality holds immediately. Otherwise all these
valuations, including v_p(420), are smaller than e. Write f=v_p(420).

For a row at offset s put x=N-s and c=k!/product_{v!=s}(s-v), a signed
nonzero integer, with the product over v=0,...,k-1. The exact equation is
  a = (x/c) product_{v!=s}(1+x/(s-v)).
Each factor in the product has p-adic valuation zero, because P divides x
and e>v_p(s-v). Thus v_p(x)=e+v_p(c). With z=a/420 and u=x/(420c),
both variables have valuation e-f>0 and satisfy
  E(u) = u product_{v!=s}(1+D_v u) = z,   D_v=420c/(s-v) in Z.

There is a unique series U in z Z[[z]] satisfying E(U)=z, by successive
comparison of coefficients, since E begins with u. Take its truncation
U_h of degree <h. At the given z, E(U_h(z))-z is divisible by
p^(h(e-f)) in the p-adic integers. For any two p-divisible elements u,v,
the quotient (E(u)-E(v))/(u-v) is a polynomial in u,v congruent to 1
mod p. Hence u=U_h(z) modulo p^(h(e-f)) for the actual solution. This
also proves x=420c U_h(z) modulo the same power. Working with the
localization of Z at p suffices throughout.

2. The local polynomial certificates

Erdos849LocalInterpolation.json lists one integer polynomial F(x,y) per
offset pair (s,r), in the two shifted rows x=n-s and y=m-r. Each entry
lists a maximum weight, a vanishing order h, and the coefficients for
monomials x^i y^j in increasing weight w=7i+8j, with 0<=i<8, 0<=j<7,
and 0<w<=the listed maximum. The weights here are distinct. The
certificate discovery used weighted lattice reduction. Its coefficients
can be verified directly using only integer arithmetic.

The checker substitutes both truncated shifted-row series from section 1
and verifies F=0 modulo z^h. Thus for the integer F evaluated at the
actual shifted rows, p^(h(e-f)) divides F(x,y). The orders are 14 or 15.

3. Archimedean bound and nonvanishing

Put T=(a/X)^(1/56)>=1. If R_k=floor((k!X)^(1/k)), then
  (R_k-s) T^(56/k) <= N-s <= (R_k+k-s) T^(56/k).
Indeed N-k+1 <= (k!a)^(1/k) <= N. Write L_x,U_x,L_y,U_y for these
positive constant endpoints in the two rows. Each monomial x^i y^j is
between L_x^i L_y^j T^w and U_x^i U_y^j T^w.

Choose the sign epsilon of the term of largest weight of F. For
epsilon F use the lower endpoints on terms of positive coefficient and
the upper endpoints on terms of negative coefficient. This gives a
polynomial lower bound G(T). The checker expands G(1+V) and verifies
that its constant is positive and all its coefficients are nonnegative.
Consequently F(x,y) is nonzero at every candidate throughout a>=X.

For an absolute upper bound, let H be the larger of the positive and
negative coefficient sums of F, taken in absolute value and weighted by
U_x^i U_y^j. Then |F(x,y)| <= H*T^W, with W its largest occupied weight.
All 56 entries pass W/(56h)<=1/16 and H^(1/h)<Q=46862860.
Divisibility and nonvanishing now give
  p^e < p^f Q (a/X)^(1/16).
If a has at most sixteen distinct prime factors, multiplying the bounds
gives a < 420 Q^16 (a/X), since the product of the p^f divides 420.
The verified strict inequality 420 Q^16<X contradicts this.

In fact W/(56h)<=11/196 for every polynomial. This gives at least
eighteen distinct primes when a>=10^238, since 17*11<196 and
  (420 Q^17)^196 * 10^((238-128)*17*11) < 10^(238*196).

Run python Erdos849LocalInterpolation.py beside the JSON data to verify
all 56 expansions, signs, weights, absolute bounds, and the final cutoff.
