# A prime divisor of at least 193

If a value $a$ occurs in columns $3,5,7,8$ or $5,6,7,8$, its largest prime divisor is at least 193. Both patterns require at least 40 distinct primes. There are 43 primes up to 191. If these supplied every prime divisor of $a$, at most three could be missing. The four-column height bounds would give $10^{137}<a<10^{250}$, since from the upper endpoint the two patterns need at least 46 and 48 primes respectively. [Forty-prime result](Erdos849FiveEightRawBranches.md) · [Height bounds](Erdos849PositionEntropy.md) · [Lower endpoint](Erdos849FourHostPairs.md)

Consider the eight factors $n-s$ of $8!a=(n)_8$. Each receives at least five primes by the five–eight coverage. With at most 43 prime factors, at most three positions can receive six or more. The four primes through seven occupy at most four positions. Consequently there is a position receiving exactly five primes, all greater than seven. Its term is the product of their full powers times a cofactor dividing 840.

To see the cofactor bound, take a prime $p$. If $p>7$, at most one of the eight terms is divisible by $p$, so it contributes no cofactor. For $p\le7$ with a valuation at our position at most $\lfloor\log_p 7\rfloor$, the contribution divides $\operatorname{lcm}(1,\ldots,7)=420$. If its valuation is larger, that term is the unique maximum. If $p$ divided $a$, it would have been assigned here, contrary to our choice of position. Thus $p\nmid a$, and the valuation of our term equals $v_p(8!)-v_p(s!(7-s)!)=v_p(8\binom7s)$. The least common multiple of 420 and all eight values $8\binom7s$ is 840.

Every one of the five powers is at most $35519(a/10^{128})^{1/36}$ by the existing four-column certificates. Enumerate their five distinct bases from 11 to 191 in increasing order and the bounded positive exponents. For each of the 16,760,778 choices of the first four powers, the verifier uses bit sets for all possible fifth powers and divisors of 840. If $q>7$ divides $a$, the term $x=n-s$ must have $(x+s)\bmod q<8$ for its actual offset $s$. Filter first with a potentially different offset at each $q$. The verifier partitions 36 test primes into four disjoint sets. At least one set consists entirely of primes dividing $a$, so keeping the union of the four sets of matches loses no candidate. The filter retained 281,369 completions. Checking the row range, then requiring the same offset at every prime except at most three, left zero rows.
