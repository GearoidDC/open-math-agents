Finiteness for repeated binomial coefficients with few distinct prime factors

Let H(a) count the pairs (n,k) of natural numbers with 1 ≤ k ≤ n/2 and
C(n,k) = a. Let ω(a) count the distinct prime factors of a.

Theorem. For each fixed integer r ≥ 0, only finitely many integers a ≥ 2
satisfy both ω(a) ≤ r and H(a) ≥ 3. Consequently, if a_j tends to infinity
and H(a_j) ≥ 3 for all j, then ω(a_j) tends to infinity.

Proof. The case r = 0 is empty. Fix r ≥ 1 and put t = r+1. Suppose
a = C(n,k) with ω(a) ≤ r. For a prime p dividing a, Legendre's formula
expresses its exponent in a as the sum over j ≥ 1 of

    floor(n/p^j) - floor(k/p^j) - floor((n-k)/p^j).

Each term is 0 or 1, and all terms with p^j > n vanish. Thus the largest
power of p dividing a is at most n. Multiplying over the primes gives
a ≤ n^r. Abbott, Erdős and Hanson use the same estimate in the proof of
their Theorem 2. https://www.renyi.hu/~p_erdos/1974-23.pdf

If t ≤ k ≤ n/2, monotonicity along the left half of a row gives

    n^r ≥ a ≥ C(n,t) = ∏_{i=0}^{t-1} (n-i)/(t-i) ≥ (n/t)^t.

The last inequality holds factor by factor. Since t = r+1, we obtain
n ≤ t^t and a ≤ t^(tr). Every representation of any larger value with
ω(a) ≤ r must therefore have column in {1,...,r}.

The edge k = 1 contributes exactly once. For each k ≥ 2, the function
n ↦ C(n,k) is strictly increasing on n ≥ k. Thus H(a) ≥ 3 supplies two
distinct columns i,j with 2 ≤ i < j ≤ r whenever a > t^(tr).

For each fixed pair 2 ≤ i < j, the equation C(n,i) = C(m,j) has finitely
many integral solutions with n ≥ 2i and m ≥ 2j. To check the hypotheses,
translate X = n-i+1 and Y = m-j+1. The equation becomes

    X(X+1)...(X+i-1) = (i!/j!) Y(Y+1)...(Y+j-1).

Theorems 2.1 and 2.2 of Beukers, Shorey and Tijdeman give an absolutely
irreducible curve of positive genus at this factorial ratio. For unequal
degrees their sole reducible case is (2,4) at ratio 1/4, whereas 2!/4!
is 1/12. Their unequal-degree genus-zero exceptions have irrational or
negative ratios. Siegel's theorem on integral points now gives the stated
finiteness. The union over the finitely many column pairs is finite, and
so is the remaining interval a ≤ t^(tr). This proves the theorem.
https://doi.org/10.1515/9783110285581.11

Effectivity. The explicit threshold t^(tr) eliminates every column above
r. The general fixed-column argument by Siegel gives no explicit maximum
value for the remaining collisions. Matomäki, Radziwiłł, Shao, Tao and
Teräväinen make this distinction in Remark 1.5. Effective classifications
are available for particular pairs, including the nine pairs in Theorem 2.2
of Blokhuis, Brouwer and de Weger.
https://arxiv.org/pdf/2106.03335
https://arxiv.org/pdf/1707.06893

There is also an effective upper bound on the number of exceptions. Let
D = max(4,r), and let J(i,j) count common entries in the two left-half
columns. The proof above bounds the total number by

    t^(tr) + Σ_{2 ≤ i < j ≤ D} J(i,j).

Leshem and Sayag's November 2026 preprint gives an explicit bound for the
sum in equation (32), using Rémond's rational-point count in genus at least
two and the solved pairs (2,3) and (2,4). Locating the final exceptional
value would require information on the heights of these points.
https://arxiv.org/pdf/2611.34736
