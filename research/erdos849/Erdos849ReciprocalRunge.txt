Simultaneous binomial columns and the reciprocal value

Let f_k(x) = x(x-1)...(x-k+1)/k!. Let M(a) count the rows n admitting
f_k(n)=a with 1<=k<=n/2, for integers a>=2. Call k>=2 proper. Each
admissible column gives exactly one row, and strict increase across the
first half of a row makes the rows for different columns distinct.
Let w=omega(a) count the distinct rational primes dividing a.

Fixed-column theorem. Suppose a occurs in distinct proper columns
k_1,...,k_r. Put d=max k_i and P=product k_i. At least one of the
following inequalities holds, with natural logarithms:

  w >= P,
  log a <= w^2 * 2^(15r+4) * (d+1)^(14r) * ((log(d!))^2+800).       (1)

The geometric argument below derives the full product P. The arithmetic
height inequality used in (1) is a specialization of Dèbes's Theorem 1.4
and the Addition immediately following it. The prime divisors of a may
vary. An independent application of the qualitative, effective
Bombieri-Sprindzuk theorem gives the same conclusion with an unspecified
computable height bound. These are ordinary mathematical arguments.

1. Critical values of a single column

All k-1 finite critical points are real and simple. Indeed, Rolle's
theorem gives one in every open gap (j,j+1), 0<=j<=k-2, exhausting the
degree of f'_k. Its absolute critical value is the maximum of |f_k| in
the closed gap, attained in the interior because the endpoints are roots.
Reflection across c=(k-1)/2 changes f_k by the sign (-1)^k.

Moving a gap one place from the left toward the center strictly lowers
its maximum, up to the equality between reflected gaps. On corresponding
interior points the ratio |f_k(x+1)/f_k(x)|=(x+1)/(k-1-x) is <1 when
x+1<k/2. This condition holds throughout each required open gap on the
left half. Comparing at a maximizing interior point of the shifted gap
gives strictness. Hence the greatest absolute critical value b_k is
attained in (0,1) and its reflection. In odd degree the signed critical
values are all distinct. In even degree the central critical point is
unpaired and the other critical points occur in mirror pairs with the
same signed value.

Moreover b_{k+1}<b_k. On (0,1) we have |f_{k+1}(x)/f_k(x)|=(k-x)/(k+1)
<1. Apply this at the maximizer for degree k+1. It follows that a signed
outer critical value of f_k is a regular value of f_l for every l>k.
This uses the normalization by k!. It does not require all finite branch
sets of different columns to be disjoint.

2. Inertia at an outer critical value

Consider the permutations of the k inverse sheets induced by loops in
the complex t-line avoiding the branch values of f_k(x)=t. The
monodromy is transitive since the covering source, a complex line with
finitely many points removed, is connected.

If k is odd, every finite critical value has one simple critical point,
so its small loop is a transposition. The finite critical loops
generate the monodromy (the loop at infinity is an inverse product).
A transitive permutation group generated by transpositions is S_k:
its transposition graph is connected. Thus the normal subgroup
generated by an outer inertia transposition acts transitively.

If k=2, its sole finite critical value gives the transposition on two
sheets. Now let k=2h with h>=2. Reflection gives f_k(x)=g((x-c)^2)
with deg g=h. The h-1 noncentral pairs of simple critical points of f_k
give h-1 distinct simple critical values of g by section 1. Indeed the
square map has nonzero derivative there, and these exhaust deg g'.
At c the critical point of f_k is simple, so g'(0) is nonzero. Also
g(0) is distinct from the other critical values. The covering by f_k
has h blocks, each consisting of the two square roots, and the monodromy
on the blocks is S_h by the transposition argument for g. Loops around
the extra value g(0) act trivially on blocks, so removing that value
from the base leaves the full block image S_h.

An inertia loop about g(0) flips just one block. Conjugation by the
transitive group on blocks provides the independent flip of every
individual block. The monodromy is contained in the group of signed
block permutations, and it contains the full block-flip kernel and
surjects onto S_h. It is therefore the full group C_2^h semidirect S_h.

Let tau denote an outer inertia element. Its block action exchanges
two blocks A,B. It is a product of two transpositions on the sheets
and fixes sheets outside these blocks. The image of its normal closure
contains all block transpositions, hence is transitive on blocks.
Writing e_A for the individual flip, this normal closure also contains
tau e_A tau^{-1} e_A^{-1}=e_B e_A. This element interchanges the two
sheets in A. Conjugation gives the corresponding within-block mobility
in every block. An orbit consequently contains both sheets of every
block it meets, and meets every block. This proves transitivity also
when h=2, where there are just two blocks and their joint flip.

3. Geometric irreducibility of every distinct-column fiber product

Over C remove infinity and all finite branch values of the chosen
polynomials. The generic fiber of the product cover is the Cartesian
product of the k_i-element sheet sets. Choose the smallest column k.
A loop around its outer critical value is trivial on every larger
column by section 1. Its component on k is tau. The projection of the
joint monodromy to the k-th monodromy is onto: removing additional
regular values still allows the loops generating the original group.
Conjugating the joint element (tau,1,...,1) thus gives a subgroup
fixing every other coordinate and acting transitively on the k sheets,
by section 2.

The projection onto all larger columns is their full joint monodromy,
for the same punctured-line reason. By induction this projection is
transitive on the product of their sheet sets. To move one full tuple
to another, first match all larger coordinates, then match the
smallest coordinate using the subgroup just constructed. The full
product cover is connected. Being smooth over this open base, it is
geometrically irreducible. Its smooth projective normalization has
degree P=product k_i over the t-line. The monic equations defining
the affine fiber product make its coordinate algebra free over Q[t]
of rank P, so there are no additional vertical curve components.

For a separate pairwise check, Rakaczki's Theorem 4 classifies
reducibility of the two falling-factorial equation over C. With
j<k, shift zero, and multiplier lambda=j!/k!, the only possible
exception has (j,k)=(2,4) and 4 lambda=1. Here lambda=1/12, so the
condition fails. Thus every distinct pair curve is geometrically
irreducible of degree jk over t. Even without the induction above,
any component of a multiple-column curve maps dominantly to every
pair curve, giving lcm_{i<j}(k_i k_j) | deg(component over t).
Pairwise irreducibility alone would not justify the full product.

4. Rational poles at zero and the varying primes

Over t=0, the fiber comprises all tuples 0<=x_i<k_i, each rational.
The derivative f'_{k_i}(x_i) is nonzero at each simple root.
Consequently the cover is unramified at all P tuples, which yield P
distinct rational poles of the reciprocal function 1/t. All admissible
integer points are smooth as well, since f'_k(x)>0 for x> k-1.

At a value a>=2, the rational number 1/a has absolute value >1 at
exactly the w finite places p|a and at no archimedean place. Thus the
Bombieri-Sprindzuk version of Runge, in Beukers and Tengely's Theorem
1.3, applies to all these points simultaneously whenever w is bounded
by W<P. Its hypothesis counts the places for each point separately.
It gives an effective finite set even when the identities of the
primes vary.

For the explicit constant, set T=1/t and

  P_i(T,Y)=T * Y(Y-1)...(Y-k_i+1) - k_i!.

At T=infinity write u=1/T. The integral local equation obtained by
clearing the pole is (Y)_{k_i}-u k_i!. Its discriminant at u=0 is
nonzero and all roots are rational. These are the separability and
unramified specialization hypotheses of Dèbes's Theorem 1.4 and
its Addition, condition (11). Each simple root lifts uniquely to a
series in Q[[u]], so the local coefficient fields are rational too.
In his notation D_infty^+=1. At the other specialization, a>=2 is
outside the branch locus because every finite critical value has
absolute value at most b_2=1/8 by section 1.
Every generic root tuple generates degree P by section 3, so D_gen=P.
The admissible specialized roots x_i are rational and distinct:
in the allowed half of the same row the binomial coefficients strictly
increase with k. Hence D_{1/a}=1. The logarithmic height h(1/a)=log a.

The coefficient vector of P_i is primitive since the coefficient of
TY^{k_i} is 1. Its largest coefficient has absolute value k_i!:
the sum of absolute coefficients of (Y)_{k_i} is product(1+j)=k_i!,
and the separate constant term has this absolute value. Thus
h(P_i)<=log(d!) and deg P_i<=d+1. Dèbes gives
s D_infty^+ D_{1/a} >= D_gen when

  log a > s^2 * 2^(15r+4) * (d+1)^(14r) * ((log(d!))^2+800).

Taking s=w gives (1). In particular the bound is independent of which
primes divide a. Degree P can also be read directly as the number of
rational poles in the preceding Runge argument.

5. Consequences for multiplicity

For every fixed positive W, all sufficiently large a with w<=W have
all proper columns <=W, with an effective threshold. Kummer's carries
formula gives v_p(binomial(n,k))<=floor(log_p n), hence each full
prime power in a is <=n, and a<=n^W. Put
N=max(2(W+1),2^(W+1)*(W+1)!+1). If n>=N then
C(n,W+1)>n^W by the falling-factorial product, since each factor
is at least n/2. For a>N^W every representing row has n>N.
Monotonicity up to half the row now excludes every k>=W+1.

There are only finitely many column subsets in {2,...,W}. Apply (1)
with the actual w to each such subset and take the maximum of the
right-hand height bounds with w replaced by W. Beyond this computable
height the entire set of proper indices satisfies product k <= w.
Since the j-th smallest proper index is at least j+1, we get M(a)!<=w.
For W=1 the product of the empty set is 1 and M(a)=1 in this range.

For example, given the nine classified pairs above 10^100 from the
accompanying work, four proper columns have product at least 840.
If the smallest is 2, the others start at 7,9,10. If it is 3 they
start at 5,7,8. If it is 4 they start at 5,7,9. If it is at least 5,
the product is at least 5*6*7*8. The tuple (3,5,7,8) attains 840
as a combinatorially admissible pattern. This shows that a sufficiently
large five-row value must have at least 840 prime divisors with
an effective height threshold. For instance, at a >= 10^(10^197),
the assumption w<=839 first bounds k<=839 by the preceding paragraph.
For any four chosen proper columns take d<=839 and log(d!)<6000.
The upper bound in (1) for log a is then at most
839^2 * 2^64 * 840^56 * (6000^2+800) < 10^197, a contradiction.

Choosing a smaller subset of the four columns improves the explicit
height cutoff to 10^(10^115). Write the sorted tuple as i<j<k<d.
The published exclusions give j>=5 and k>=7. If d>=120, the two
largest columns already have product >=840. For 24<=d<=119 the last
three suffice; for d<=23 use all four. In the three cases use
(r,D,H)=(2,841,10000), (3,121,1000), (4,25,100), respectively.
Here D strictly exceeds every chosen polynomial's total degree, and
2*d!<2^H ensures its logarithmic height is <H even using the sum
of its coefficient magnitudes. In each case

  839^2 * 2^(15r+4) * D^(14r) * (H^2+800) < 10^115.

Erdos849RungeConstants.py checks these integer comparisons and the
minimum product. The numerical cutoff is enormous. The argument
supplies no all-size bound of 840 primes.

Separately, Rakaczki's Theorem 1 gives qualitative finiteness of common
integer values of every two fixed distinct proper columns. For the
unweighted equation the exceptional coefficient test at (2,4) gives
3, which is neither 1 nor -9/16. Combining the large-a cutoff k<=W
with this result shows that only finitely many a have w<=W and M(a)>=3.
This last argument provides no general computable bound in the
remaining cases P<=W. For the full Erdős problem both the prime count
and the proper indices may grow: the preceding results neither exhibit
a missing multiplicity nor realize all positive multiplicities.

6. Why ordinary Runge at infinity cannot cover the problem

A loop about t=infinity cycles all k_i inverse branches of the i-th
polynomial. On the Cartesian product its orbits all have length
lcm(k_i). Normalization therefore gives product(k_i)/lcm(k_i) distinct
geometric places above infinity. Pairwise coprime degrees give one
place, even for arbitrarily many distinct primes as column indices.

The three patterns (3,5,7,8), (5,6,7,8), and (3,5,7,15) each have only
one rational Galois orbit of poles at infinity. The first has one
geometric place. For the second there are two, and the residue of
x_6^3/x_8^4 has square 6!/8!=1/56. For the third there are fifteen.
The residues of x_3/x_15^5 and x_5/x_15^3 have cube and fifth power
3!/15! and 5!/15!, respectively. The reciprocal radicals have degrees
3 and 5 by Eisenstein at 13, so each residue field has degree divisible
by 15. These residue formulas follow from x_k^k/(k!t) tending to 1.

The real positive inverse branches give a real place at infinity.
If all poles comprise one orbit, allowing a finite extension K of Q
still cannot meet the ordinary Runge inequality for these points.
Indeed in a common finite Galois closure let G be the Galois group,
H the stabilizer of the real pole, U the subgroup fixing K, and c
complex conjugation. Since c is in H, the number of pole orbits over
K is |U\G/H|=|H\G/U| <= |<c>\G/U|=r_1(K)+r_2(K). This already
counts the archimedean places in the set S needed for ordinary
S-integrality. The reciprocal method instead uses the split fiber at 0.
