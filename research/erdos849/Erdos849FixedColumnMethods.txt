Effective finiteness for some unrestricted fixed column patterns

Put f_k(x)=(x)_k/k!, where (x)_k=x(x-1)...(x-k+1). A fixed pattern
means distinct specified columns with f_k(n_k)=a and n_k>=2k.
Sections 1 and 2 give effective finiteness independently of the
prime factors of a or any of its translates. Section 3 records what
additional finite fibers give when the prime support varies.

1. Columns 2 and 4

The identities

  8 f_2(n)+1=(2n-1)^2,
  24 f_4(n)+1=(n(n-3)+1)^2

turn a simultaneous representation into an integral hyperelliptic
equation. If the other column is a fixed d>2 or d>4, respectively,
then 1+c f_d(x) is squarefree, with c=8 or c=24. To verify this, let
b_k be the largest absolute critical value of f_k. The critical
points are simple and interlace the consecutive roots; the ratio
|f_k(x+1)/f_k(x)|=(x+1)/(k-1-x) places the largest absolute
critical value in the outermost gaps. On (0,1),
|f_{k+1}(x)/f_k(x)|=(k-x)/(k+1)<1, so b_k decreases strictly.
For k=2 its value is 1/8. For k=4 the minimum is -1/24 by the
square identity, attained where n(n-3)+1=0 in the outermost gaps.
Hence b_d<1/c, excluding a multiple root at the level -1/c.
The remaining (3,4) case also has squarefree 1+24 f_3, since the
only critical values of f_3 are +/-1/(9 sqrt(3)).

In particular, take the polynomial F(X)=d!+c(X)_d and multiplier
b=d!. The equation F(x)=b y^2 has squarefree integral F of degree
d>=3. The hyperelliptic bound of Bérczes, Evertse and Győry,
Theorem 2.2, supplies an explicit bound for log max(1,|x|,|y|).
Their parameters are Q, S consisting only of the real place, and
coefficient height at most log(24 d!). For example the deliberately
enlarged upper bound

  (4d)^(10000 d^4) * (24 d!)^(10000 d^4)

suffices. Enumerating x up to the resulting bound would recover
every admissible intersection, with no assumption on the value's
factorization. The bound applies to each fixed d. A four-column
pattern containing 2 or 4 always includes a partner in the indicated
range. The quadratic-column case is also a classical result of
Brindza, extending Kiss's odd-prime-column case.

For clarity, the simple identity used here has a restricted scope.
If A f_k(x)+B is a nonconstant perfect m-th power in C[x], with
A nonzero and m>=2, every root must be a critical point. All critical
points are simple, so m=2 and every root is double. Each finite
critical value occurs at at most two points by the central reflection
and the strict gap-height comparison. Thus k<=4 and k is even:
the two columns above exhaust such affine power identities.

2. Rational signs at infinity from several even columns

Suppose that four distinct even column indices k1,...,k4 have square
factorial product Q^2=product(k_i!). On the normalized binomial
fiber product, the rational function

  G = x_1^(k1/2) x_2^(k2/2) x_3^(k3/2) x_4^(k4/2) / t^2

has finite nonzero residue at every place above t=infinity. Its
square has residue Q^2, since x_i^k_i/(k_i! t) has residue 1.
Both residues +Q and -Q occur. In the inverse Puiseux branches
x_i=(k_i! t)^(1/k_i) times a k_i-th root of unity to leading order,
independent choices of the sheets allow switching the sign at one
coordinate. The simultaneous inertia changes every half-power sign,
so the product of the four signs is fixed along a branch orbit.
The whole product cover is geometrically irreducible by the
monodromy proof in Erdos849ReciprocalRunge.txt. The two residues
are distinct rational numbers, so the corresponding poles of t
belong to different arithmetic Galois orbits. Applying ordinary
Runge at the single real place of Q effectively bounds ALL shared
integer values. This argument works for any nonempty even-sized
subset of even column indices whose factorial product is square.

For example (6,10,34,36) satisfies the criterion:

  (10!/6!)(36!/34!)=(7*8*9*10)(35*36)=2520^2.

There are infinitely many such patterns. For odd j>=3 write
A_j+2u_j sqrt(2)=(3+2 sqrt(2))^j. The coefficient A_j is
3 modulo 4 and A_j^2-8u_j^2=1. Thus k=(A_j-3)/2 is even and
(k+1)(k+2)=2u_j^2. Choose two distinct such indices k,l and use
the four columns (k,k+2,l,l+2). Their factorial product is square
because (k+2)!/k! and (l+2)!/l! have the same square class.

In contrast, each individual pair j<d has just one Galois orbit
of geometric points over infinity. Indeed their number is g=gcd(j,d)
by the joint inertia cycle. The ratio x_j^(j/g)/x_d^(d/g) has
g-th power residue j!/d!. If g>1 then d-j>=2. The reciprocal
d!/j! is a product of at least two consecutive positive integers
and is no perfect power by Erdős and Selfridge. Its positive g-th
root has degree g: if the minimal polynomial had degree e<g,
the absolute value of its nonzero rational constant coefficient
would be (d!/j!)^(e/g), since all conjugates have the same modulus.
This would make d!/j! a rational, hence integral, power of degree
g/gcd(e,g)>1. The positive real branch thus has residue degree g,
exhausting the whole infinity fiber in one orbit. This explains
why the square-product obstruction can first appear across several
columns. A tuple of distinct odd primes can still have only one
geometric point over infinity, for any tuple size.

3. The translates a-1 and a

The rational roots of f_k(x)=1 are exactly k and, for even k, -1.
Indeed the equation is monic integral after clearing k!, the
integer arguments in [0,k-1] are roots of f_k, and outside that
interval monotonicity and reflection settle the possibilities.
All k geometric roots are simple since the absolute critical values
are strictly less than 1. Put c_k=2 for k=2 or k odd, and c_k=3
for k even and >=4. The geometric roots of f_k(x)=1 partition into
at least c_k Galois-invariant nonempty sets, taking each rational
point separately and then the remaining roots if present. Thus
for any r fixed proper columns the fiber above t=1 has at least
product c_k rational Galois orbits. The generalized Runge theorem
applied to 1/(t-1) effectively bounds the common values a>=2 if
omega(a-1)<product c_k. This is independent of omega(a), but
still needs a condition on a-1. Using 1/[t(t-1)] yields at least
product k + product c_k pole orbits; since gcd(a,a-1)=1, the prime
support counts add as well.

For a concrete limitation on a local strategy, any finite set of
congruence tests admits arbitrarily large integers a for which
both a and a-1 have arbitrarily many additional prime factors and
every tested binomial column is soluble locally at the prescribed
primes. Near t=0 the root x=0 of each f_k is simple over Q_p;
near t=1 the same holds at x=k. Choose a close enough to 0 at the
tested primes, close to 0 at any prescribed number of new large
primes, and close to 1 at disjoint new large primes. Hensel lifting
and the Chinese remainder theorem give the stated local solvability.
This constructs local data, not a simultaneous integer representation.

There is also an obstruction to obtaining connected étale cyclic
covers by adjoining radicals of the individual linear factors at
these split fibers. Over the algebraic closure, at any unramified
fiber t=b, the divisor of x_i-s for a root s of f_i(s)=b has
valuation one along the tuples with coordinate x_i=s and zero at
the other tuples. For the product with exponents u_(i,s) to have
all valuations multiples of m, the sum over i of u_(i,s_i) must
vanish modulo m for every tuple (s_i). Varying one coordinate
shows u_(i,s) is constant modulo m in each coordinate i, and the
sum of those constants is zero modulo m. But product_s(x_i-s)
equals k_i! (t-b), so the original product is a constant times
an m-th power in the function field. The same argument applies
separately to any finite collection of unramified fibers. More
general functions or noncyclic covers require separate analysis.
